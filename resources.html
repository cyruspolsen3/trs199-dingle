The desert tradition arrived in Ireland not through geography but through text — via John Cassian's Conferences, copied in monastic scriptoria from Gaul to Kerry. The díseart of the Dingle Peninsula is the Egyptian desert translated into Atlantic stone. These resources trace that translation.
Primary source · Desert tradition
The Anonymous Sayings of the Desert Fathers
Trans. John Wortley · Internet Archive
Free · Online
The anonymous layer of the Apophthegmata Patrum — sayings not attributed to a named Abba or Amma but preserved by their living wisdom. Wortley's translation is scholarly and readable. The desert fathers and mothers spoke in short, precise bursts — a single sentence meant to inhabit a disciple for weeks. Read slowly, not comprehensively. The exercise on Day 1 (threshold attention) and Day 2 (enclosure, Abba Moses on the cell) both draw directly from this collection.

Primary source · Desert Mothers
Amma Syncletica, Theodora, and Sarah: Sayings and Commentary
VK McCarty · Public Orthodoxy (2020) · Aleteia (2017) · Axia Women
Free · Online
Three women whose sayings were preserved in the Apophthegmata Patrum alongside those of the Abbas. Amma Syncletica opens the daily exercises because she spoke most directly about the sea: her ship-and-storm saying is the closest thing the desert tradition has to a maritime spirituality. Amma Theodora on the storm that precedes the fruit; Amma Sarah on sixty years of prayer beside a river she never stopped to watch. All three are available across the three linked sources below. Read the sayings slowly — one or two per day — rather than all at once.

Primary source · Navigatio
The Voyage of Saint Brendan — Summary Retelling
Irish Folklore Centre · Based on O'Meara translation (1976)
Free · Online
The clearest popular retelling of the Navigatio Sancti Brendani Abbatis — the 7th-century Irish monk's seven-year circular voyage to the Promised Land of the Saints, recorded in 10th-century Latin. 125 manuscripts survive, evidence of its extraordinary reach. Essential reading before Day 5 (Brandon Creek), where you will stand at the departure point. Two things to hold: the voyage is circular — Brendan is instructed to return home before he can die; and the whale Jasconius, mistaken for an island every Easter, is the course's central image of misreading — we build fires on what we think is solid ground, and the ground moves.

Contemporary magisterium · pilgrimage · AI era
Pope Leo XIV — Magnifica Humanitas (2026)
Vatican · 15 May 2026 · First social encyclical of Leo XIV
Free · Vatican
Released on 15 May 2026 — during the first week of this course — Magnifica Humanitas is Pope Leo XIV’s first major social encyclical. Two passages are directly relevant to the course.

Paragraph 15 names the Ordinary Jubilee Year of 2025 as “a pilgrimage of hope” and addresses the era of artificial intelligence directly: “In the era of artificial intelligence, when human dignity is threatened by new forms of dehumanization, ours is the pressing duty to remain profoundly human. We must lovingly safeguard the grandeur of humanity bestowed upon us… True progress always stems from a heart open to others, an intelligence willing to listen and a will that seeks what unites rather than what separates.”

Paragraph 213 quotes J.R.R. Tolkien — the same author whose Ring of Fire passage opens this course on Day 1: “It is not our part to master all the tides of the world, but to do what is in us for the succour of those years wherein we are set, uprooting the evil in the fields that we know, so that those who live after may have clean earth to till.” The encyclical frames this as the foundation of “small and steadfast acts of fidelity that serve as a bulwark against dehumanization” — a description equally applicable to Rob Fell’s Iveragh Woodlands work, Billy Mag Fhloinn’s Irish names for nature, and the monks at Kilmalkedar carving the alphabet into stone.

One student in this course, walking the Commons of Dingle on the day the encyclical was released, said the surrounding landscape reminded her of the Lord of the Rings. She was not wrong. The course and the encyclical are in the same conversation.

Scholarly context · Pilgrimage geometries
Three Pilgrims Searching for Home: The Navigatio, Sigeric, and The Wanderer
Sandra Anne Jones · MA Thesis · English and Linguistics Programme · 2023
Scholarly · Thesis
A rigorous 123-page comparative study of three 10th-century pilgrimage texts, identifying three recurring characteristics — identity, time and space, and memory — across very different forms. Jones's central contribution for this course is the three-geometry framework: Brendan's voyage as the cycle (circular, anchored by the monastic calendar), Sigeric's itinerary as the line (strict, horizontal, the land a measurable barrier), The Wanderer's exile as the void (aimless drift, resolved only by vertical ascension). This framework is the structural tool for the Day 8 Lightning Talk comparative exercise. Jones's thesis grew out of her own pilgrimage along the Via Francigena to Rome — she writes as both scholar and pilgrim. Properly cited: Jones, Sandra Anne. "Three Pilgrims Searching for Home." MA Thesis, English and Linguistics Programme, 2023.
Available via Dr. Olsen Days 5, 8
Practitioner scholarship · pilgrimage research synthesis · Lightning Talk preparation
Sacred Design Lab — Spiritual Innovation Briefing: Pilgrimage
spiritualinnovation.org · Sacred Design Lab · 2025–2026
Free · Online
A practitioner-oriented research synthesis from the Sacred Design Lab, drawing on peer-reviewed scholarship across the Camino de Santiago, Kumano Kodo, and Hajj. Well-sourced and readable for a general-population undergraduate. Four contributions are directly relevant to this course.

Van Gennep’s three-phase framework (separation, transition, incorporation) is the structural backbone of the briefing — and of this course. Separation: arrival in Dingle, the harbor walk, the first field observation. Transition: the Cosán, the Blaskets, the music sessions, the Blessing of the Hands — the seven days in which identity becomes, as Victor and Edith Turner put it, “betwixt and between.” Incorporation: the June 3 Closing Circle, the Lightning Talks, and the post-return writing. Naming this framework gives you a structure for understanding why the course is sequenced the way it is.

Kurrat’s five pilgrim typologies are immediately useful for Lightning Talk preparation: the balance pilgrim (seeking silence and reflection later in life); the crisis pilgrim (making sense of a disruptive event); the time-out pilgrim (looking for a reset during burnout); the transitional pilgrim (marking a life change); and the new-start pilgrim (striving for a fresh start). Students can identify themselves in these categories, which is a more honest entry point into comparative pilgrimage analysis than abstract theology. Use this alongside Jones (2023)’s cycle / line / void framework for the Lightning Talk.

The Kumano Kodo awe research introduces the kataribe — a local guide trained in cultural history and environmental stewardship whose presence significantly deepens pilgrims’ experience of awe by fostering interpretation and meaning. Rob Fell is a kataribe. Billy Mag Fhloinn is a kataribe. This vocabulary names what you have already encountered on the course.

Tip 3: expect modest changes rather than miracles. The incorporation research on the Hajj is the most pastorally honest passage in the document: family and work obligations crowd out religious commitments on return, and this tension underscores the ongoing nature of self-transformation rather than its failure. This pairs directly with Patrick Beldio’s note in the Gallarus field card: “if you stepped inside and felt nothing in particular, write that. It is a legitimate response.” The same honesty applies to what happens after you return home.

The briefing also covers virtual pilgrimage, labyrinth walking, and pilgrimage-as-reading as alternate routes — relevant to the course’s AI Audit Trail discussions about what digital mediation does to embodied experience.

Primary source · Old English elegy
The Wanderer — Translation and Commentary
Various translators · Exeter Book · c. 960–970
Free · Online
A 115-line Old English elegy preserved in the Exeter Book — the same manuscript that contains over 30 poems and 90 riddles, bequeathed to Exeter Cathedral in the 11th century. The poem traces the interior pilgrimage of a solitary thegn cast into exile after his lord's death, rowing on a frost-cold sea, haunted by hallucinations of the hall he has lost. Its central movement is the ubi sunt motif: Hwær cwom mearg? Hwær cwom mago? — "Where has the horse gone? Where the rider?" This is the voice of the Blasket Islands viewed from Slea Head. Read before Day 3. The Exeter Book digital facsimile is available online — the actual manuscript page, written by a single scribe, is worth seeing alongside the translation.

Ignatian formation · Gallagher
'Enlarging the Mind': Michael Paul Gallagher SJ and the Imagination
Nicolas Steeves SJ · Ignaziana: Rivista di Ricerca Teologica
Print · Packet
The intellectual framework of the daily exercises draws substantially from Michael Paul Gallagher SJ (1939–2015), Irish Jesuit, literary scholar, and theologian at the Pontifical Gregorian University in Rome. Steeves's memorial essay traces Gallagher's method: making unlike things sit down and listen to each other — Thérèse de Lisieux and Nietzsche, Rahner and Rilke — until something genuine passes between them. His governing principle, drawn from Newman, was enlarging the mind; his governing gesture, from Luke 5:4, was duc in altum — put out into the deep. For secular students: Gallagher's approach does not require theological assent. It requires only a willingness to take seriously what you encounter. Key works: Dive Deeper: The Human Poetry of Faith (2001); Clashing Symbols (1997); Faith Maps (2010).

Ignatian · Daily examen
How to Do the Examen Prayer
Ignatian Spirituality · IgnatianSpirituality.com
Free · Online
A plain-language introduction to the daily examen — the five-step review of the day that structures the evening section of the daily exercises. The examen is not a guilt exercise. It is a form of honest self-observation: where did my attention open today, and where did it close? Ignatius called these movements consolación and desolación — not emotions, but directions of energy. This resource introduces the practice clearly for students with no prior Ignatian background. Read before Day 1. The version used in the exercises is adapted for the field context — you do not need a chapel, a candle, or any particular posture. You need five minutes and honesty.

Poetry · North Atlantic vernacular
"Beachcomber"
George Mackay Brown · from Fishermen with Ploughs (Hogarth Press, 1971)
Print · Packet
The poem that opens the daily exercises and establishes the North Atlantic vernacular for the whole course. Seven stanzas, one per day of the week, each recording what the sea returned to the beachcomber. Plain language. Precise observation. The sacred held in material images without announcement: Tuesday's timber that "next winter will be a chair, a coffin, a bed"; Friday's skull with sand spilling from it "the way time is told on kirkyard stones"; Sunday's question — "What's heaven? A sea chest with a thousand gold coins." Mackay Brown (1921–1996) lived his entire life in Orkney, a North Atlantic archipelago structurally identical to the Kerry coast. He converted to Catholicism in 1961. Read this poem once before departure. Read it again standing on the shore at Slea Head. Note what is different. © Estate of George Mackay Brown / John Murray. Reproduced by permission in course materials.

Prose · Story and Fable
The Story and the Fable — Selected Passages
Edwin Muir · Autobiography (1954) · Excerpts in course reader
Print · Packet
Edwin Muir (1887–1959) grew up on the island of Wyre in Orkney — a North Atlantic upbringing that gave him the same landscape intuition as Mackay Brown. His Autobiography contains the governing framework for the course's journaling practice: the distinction between Story (what happens in a life) and Fable (the deeper pattern beneath what happens). Muir could not write the Fable directly — he could only notice the moments when it briefly surfaced through the Story. The pilgrimage is one of those moments. Two passages are essential: the Glasgow tramcar experience (the terror of seeing fellow passengers as animals — the cost of fully materialist thinking) and the Achilles-Hector poem (written thirty years after a childhood shame, which the poem then cleared). The second passage is the argument for why the field journal matters.

Poetry · Irish literary tradition
Poetry Portal — Eight Poems for Memorization
Heaney · Ó Tuama · Boland · Ní Dhomhnaill · Montague · O'Donohue
Free · Online
The companion portal contains all eight course poems with contextual notes, four-step memorization sequences, cloze exercises, recall scoring, and AI audit trail forms. The poems are not decoration — they are a form of knowing available to the body that the analytical essay cannot replicate. Heaney's "The Peninsula" enacts the course's central paradox: "you will not arrive / but pass through, though always skirting landfall." His "Postscript" (often called Heaney's perfect poem) describes the Atlantic light working off the ocean and the earthed lightning of swans — the Ignatian consolación sin causa in lyric form. Ní Dhomhnaill's bilingual "An Crann" holds the tension of two languages that cannot fully translate each other — the condition of every student in this landscape.

Poetry · Living Irish-language voice
Simon Ó Faoláin — Selected Poems (bilingual)
portraidi.ie/en/simon-o-faolain · Coiscéim, Dublin
Free · Online
Ó Faoláin writes in Irish and translates his own work into English — which means the gap between the two versions is not a translator's loss but the poet's own reckoning with what moves between languages. His poem "Back West" / "Thiar" (from As Gaineamh, Coiscéim, 2011) is set in the exact landscape of the Slea Head circuit: irregular fields, the harbour below, the empty houses with chrome combination locks on their gateposts, tost toll laistigh — hollow silence within. It is the ubi sunt of the Blaskets stated as contemporary Irish-language poetry. "Winnowing" / "Ag Cáitheadh" (from Anam Mhadra, Coiscéim, 2008) is eight lines on whether, at the last breath, it was the chaff or the grain that flew off in the wind — Muir's Story/Fable in a single image. Both poems appear in the daily exercises (Days 5 and 7). Students will encounter Ó Faoláin in person on Day 5 of the course.

Living music · Dingle Peninsula · Day 4
Rob Fell — Uilleann Pipes, Irish Flute & Traditional Music
An Díseart, Dingle · Iveragh Woodlands EIP Co-ordinator · Originally from Firies, Co. Kerry
Day 4 · In person
Rob Fell is a celebrated traditional Irish musician, uilleann piper, and flute player based on the Dingle Peninsula. He learned his music from the renowned Buckley brothers of Kerry — Pádraig and Tadhg — and performs regularly at An Díseart in Dingle, a cultural and spiritual centre whose very name (díseart: hermitage) connects the living music tradition to the monastic landscape of the course. His style draws on historic piping recordings and traditional flute players from Connacht and County Clare.

Fell is also the Iveragh Woodlands EIP Co-ordinator, working with local farmers to regenerate upland ecologies, and teaches children the Irish names for the natural world — Ainmneacha Gaeilge don Dúlra. This dual life as musician and ecologist is the course's most direct embodiment of the Celtic understanding that landscape, language, and music are a single continuous reality — John Moriarty's "walking beautifully on the earth" enacted as a vocation. He teaches tin whistle and flute at events like Scoil Cheoil na hEarráigh, and his work has been covered by The Advertiser and featured on Féile na Bealtaine.

The uilleann pipes are played sitting, bellows-driven, with a harmonic complexity unlike any other bagpipe tradition in the world. Students who have never heard them will find the experience unlike anything they have encountered. Listen before you analyze.

Field companion · Gallarus Oratory · Day 5
Gallarus Oratory: A Companion for the Threshold
Written by Patrick Beldio, University of Scranton, for TRS 199 · Dingle 2026
Print · Field card
A pastoral companion text for the Gallarus Oratory enclosure experience on Day 5, written in the spirit of John Dunne’s “passing over and coming back.” Two versions: a lean version for reading at the threshold, and a fuller version for those who want more context before or after the visit.

The lean version is also available as a printable single-page field card — designed to be folded into the field journal and carried to the door. The card includes the full companion text, ruled lines for a field journal entry, and a closing note for students who felt nothing inside the oratory: If you stepped inside and felt nothing in particular, write that. Honestly. It is a legitimate response and the journey does not require you to perform an experience you did not have. Sometimes a place does not speak to us on first encounter. Sometimes it speaks later, when we are somewhere else entirely and least expecting it.

Fuller version — read before or after the visit:

Gallarus Oratory has stood on this hillside above Smerwick Harbour for somewhere between one thousand and thirteen hundred years. No one knows exactly when it was built or by whom. What we know is that people came here deliberately, to this edge of the Atlantic, to be enclosed in stone and silence. The building has never collapsed. It has never needed restoration. It has simply held its shape, through every century, waiting.

You are going to enter it one at a time, or in very small groups, and spend at least ten minutes inside. There is no program for what happens in there.

Before you enter: Pause at the door. It is low and narrow. You will have to slow down and lower your head to pass through. Let that be intentional rather than just physical. You are crossing a threshold. Something is being asked of you, even if you cannot yet say what. Take a breath before you go in.

Inside: Let yourself arrive before you do anything else. Feel the stone around you. Notice the quality of the light from the single window and the single door. Notice the temperature, the smell, the silence, or the sound of wind or birds if it reaches you. Take your time with this. There is no right way to receive a place.

When you feel settled, bring your attention to two things you already know: that you are here, alive, present in this moment. And that someday you will not be. You do not need to dwell on the second of these with any heaviness. Just let both be true at the same time, the way they always are, even when we are not paying attention.

Now bring the question that is actually alive in you — the one you have been carrying, perhaps for years, perhaps without knowing it had a shape. You do not need to speak it or write it yet. Let it be present with you the way the light is present: without forcing anything.

Sit with your question. If insight comes, let it come. If nothing comes, let the nothing be what it is. Silence is not the absence of an answer. It is sometimes the answer itself, arriving in a form we are not yet used to receiving.

When you are ready to leave: Step back out through the low door into the light and the landscape. Write in response to one of these — choose the one that feels most alive to you right now:
“What I am carrying out of that place that I did not carry in is…”
“The question I brought in with me was… and what happened to it inside was…”
“What I noticed was…”
There are no wrong answers. The only requirement is that what you write is true.

Cartoon Saloon was founded in Kilkenny in 1999 by Tomm Moore, Nora Twomey, and Paul Young. It is the most significant body of animated work to emerge from Ireland and one of the most consistently original animation studios in the world, with multiple Academy Award nominations across its three major films. Its visual language is rooted in Celtic interlace, medieval manuscript tradition, and the living ecology of the Irish landscape. Tomm Moore has described Celtic interlace as his model for narrative structure: not linear, not hierarchical, but interwoven — every strand connecting to every other, the pattern only becoming visible when you step back far enough to see the whole. This is also a description of what this course is doing across seven days.

The three films form a trilogy in everything but name. The Secret of Kells is shown in class on May 28 (lecture hour, after the Cosán na Naomh walk). Wolfwalkers and Song of the Sea are both recommended personal viewing — see individual cards below for timing and platform.

The Secret of Kells

The film dramatizes the creation of the Book of Kells inside a monastery under Viking siege — enclosure, illuminated manuscript, Celtic interlace as a form of prayer. The film's monastery is structurally identical to what students walked through: the cashel wall, the scriptorium, the tension between the enclosed community and the hostile world outside. The Chi-Rho page that forms the film's climax is the same manuscript tradition the Kilmalkedar alphabet stone belongs to.
